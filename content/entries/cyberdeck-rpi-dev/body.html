Intent

What it is, and what it is for

A self-contained, transportable Raspberry Pi deck that runs Kali Linux for cloud-posture scanning and general lab work — a real keyboard and displays, a control surface, and enough compute to run scans without depending on a network it did not bring with it. It is meant to be set down and worked on next to its power source, not walked around.

The kit

Starting from an open design, not a blank sheet

The enclosure is RPI DEV, an open-source cyberdeck designed by SECTOR 07 — a Raspberry Pi 5 platform with dual 9″ rotating touchscreens, 42 printable STL parts plus STEP source, four support PCBs (KiCad + a PCBWay ordering pack), a BOM and an assembly guide. It is not an in-house design; the renders on this page are made from its published STL files, and full credit goes to SECTOR 07. Their own project shows the finished, assembled deck — this page documents adapting it for cloud-security work.

Reading the STL parts and the board schematics settled one thing early: this is a single-Pi deck. It has one Pi bay and one 40-pin carrier; the four boards are the deck's own keyboard, power, GPIO riser and USB expansion, not compute nodes. A signature rotary hinge carries the display on two printed arms. That single-Pi reality turned out to match the work exactly — see the right-sizing note at the end.

Verify what a file actually is

The board set is named RPI_Dev_1…4, which reads like four nodes. The schematics say otherwise: one is a switch/rotary control HAT, one a Pi carrier with a barrel jack, one a GPIO riser, one a USB board. Four PCBs, one computer.

Power

An external station removes the hard part

Rather than pack cells inside the deck, power comes from an external Anker SOLIX C1000 — a 1,024 Wh LiFePO4 station with a 2,000 W inverter and a UPS mode. Against a deck that draws roughly 50 W, that is all-day runtime plus automatic ride-through of a mains cut, with no internal battery, BMS or charger to design, age or certify.

One non-obvious detail: feed the deck from the station's DC outputs (USB-C or 12 V), not the AC outlet. Running a 2,000 W inverter to power a 50 W load wastes ~15–20 W on the inverter's own idle — up to a third of the actual draw.

Balance

The problem the external battery created

Moving the battery out of the deck removed its heaviest forward component — which was also, in a clamshell, the counterweight. A laptop tips backward when the open lid's centre of mass passes behind the rear feet, and a dual-display lid with no ballast in the base does exactly that.

The fix is a dense plate carried to the front lip of the base — position matters more than mass, because weight added at the rear makes tipping worse. Brass or steel earns its place here where aluminium does not: same volume, roughly three times the mass. The other honest fix is a lighter lid — dropping the displays from 10.1″ to 7″ cuts lid mass about 40% and does more for stability than any plate.

The lever, stated plainly

Stability is a ratio of moments about the rear feet: base mass × its distance forward, over lid mass × its distance back. Every gram of counterweight only counts for how far forward it sits.

Right-sizing

One Pi is the honest answer to the work

The temptation with a cyberdeck is to cluster several Pis for the sense of power. But the actual work — cloud-posture scanning against provider APIs — is network- and rate-limit-bound, not compute-bound. More local cores do not scan faster when the ceiling is the cloud provider's API. So the single-Pi kit is not a compromise; it is correctly sized, and the effort goes where it matters: NVMe instead of an SD card, honest cooling, and a clean power feed.

The maximal four-node version still has a place — as a portfolio piece and for learning cluster orchestration on real hardware — but it is a different machine with a different chassis, and it would not do this job any faster.
