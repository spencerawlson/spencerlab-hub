Intent

What it is, and what it is for

A self-contained, transportable Raspberry Pi deck that runs Kali Linux for cloud-posture scanning and general lab work — a real keyboard and displays, a control surface, and enough compute to run scans without depending on a network it did not bring with it. It is meant to be set down and worked on next to its power source, not walked around.

The kit

Starting from an open design, not a blank sheet

The enclosure is RPI DEV, an open-source cyberdeck designed by SECTOR 07 — a Raspberry Pi 5 platform with dual 9″ rotating touchscreens, 42 printable STL parts plus STEP source, four support PCBs (KiCad + a PCBWay ordering pack), a BOM and an assembly guide. It is not an in-house design; the renders on this page are made from its published files, and full credit goes to SECTOR 07. The design is open-source on the RPI DEV repo, and SECTOR 07's build video shows the finished, assembled deck. This page documents adapting it for cloud-security work.

Reading the STL parts and the board schematics settled one thing early: this is a single-Pi deck. It has one Pi bay and one 40-pin carrier; the four boards are the deck's own keyboard, power, GPIO riser and USB expansion, not compute nodes. A signature rotary hinge carries the display on two printed arms. That single-Pi reality turned out to match the work exactly — see the right-sizing note at the end.

Verify what a file actually is

The board set is named RPI_Dev_1…4, which reads like four nodes. The schematics say otherwise: one is a switch/rotary control HAT, one a Pi carrier with a barrel jack, one a GPIO riser, one a USB board. Four PCBs, one computer.

Power

One 5 V rail, fed rather than stored

RPI DEV runs from a single 5 V input at the base's barrel jack. The assembly calls for a supply trimmed to about 5.3 V so the carrier board sees a clean 5.1 V at the Pi. A single Pi 5 with dual 9″ screens and a powered hub is a modest load — on the order of 20–30 W — so there is no internal battery, BMS or charger in the design: the deck is fed, not self-powered.

That leaves field power as a simple, separate choice layered on top: the same 5 V rail can come from a USB-C PD bank stepped to 5 V, or from a portable power station for a long session away from mains. It changes nothing in the deck itself — only what sits at the other end of the barrel jack.

Mechanics

The hard mechanical work is already solved

Starting from a finished design means the balance and hinge problems a scratch-built clamshell would fight are already handled. Each screen rides an orange rotary-hinge disc on ball bearings, swiveling independently between landscape and portrait, and the printed arms route the display cables down to the base. The enclosure is almost entirely snap-fit — screws only where they must be — which is what keeps it printable and serviceable at home.

Why adopt rather than design

Taking a proven open design skips exactly this class of work. The mechanical R&D is done, so the effort moves to the parts that are specific to the job: the OS, the security tooling, and how it gets powered in the field.

Right-sizing

Single-Pi is the right size for this work

RPI DEV is a single-Pi deck by design, and for cloud-security work that is the correct size, not a limit. Cloud-posture scanning runs against provider APIs — it is network- and rate-limit-bound, not compute-bound — so more local cores do not scan faster once the ceiling is the provider's own API. The effort goes where it actually helps: NVMe over an SD card, honest cooling, and the Kali tooling on top.
